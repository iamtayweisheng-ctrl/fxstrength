An oil shock is usually discussed as an energy story. For currency traders, it can become something much bigger.

Higher oil prices increase the cost of importing energy, put pressure on inflation and squeeze household and business spending. Central banks then face a difficult choice: tighten policy to contain inflation, or avoid making an already weaker economy worse. That is where the currency impact becomes interesting.

The same oil shock can push one central bank towards another rate hike while making another more cautious. And even when several central banks are raising rates, their currencies do not necessarily benefit equally.

**The important question is not simply which central bank has the highest interest rate. It is which central bank is expected to tighten further — and whether its economy can withstand it.**

## The oil shock is no longer just about supply

The Strait of Hormuz remains a major focus because disruptions to shipping can threaten the flow of energy through an important global route. In the week to 15 November, the market was dealing with renewed US–Iran tensions, reports of military activity and continued uncertainty around shipping through the Strait, with WTI near **$89** and Brent above **$98** as geopolitical risk supported prices. (In the weeks since, the oil price itself has stayed surprisingly subdued even as the conflict escalated — we unpack why in [The Gulf Conflict Is Escalating. So Why Isn't Brent Rising?](/the-bigger-picture/gulf-conflict-brent-not-rising))

But the FX consequences do not stop at the oil market. When energy prices rise, importing economies face a larger bill for the same amount of energy. That can:

- Increase demand for foreign currency to pay for energy imports.
- Worsen the trade balance if export earnings don't rise alongside import costs.
- Push domestic inflation higher.
- Reduce household purchasing power and raise business costs.

For energy *exporters*, higher prices can have the opposite effect — export revenues may rise, improving the trade balance and supporting domestic income. But that doesn't automatically make every energy exporter a currency winner. The benefit depends on how much of its economy relies on energy exports, how much revenue stays within the country, and how investors read the wider outlook.

**An oil shock creates different economic pressures across countries. The currency impact depends on which pressure becomes most important.**

## Central banks are pushed the same way — but not at the same speed

The latest policy developments suggest inflation pressure is becoming a problem for several major central banks. The Federal Reserve is signalling that further tightening may be needed; the ECB and Bank of England also have additional tightening priced into expectations. Australia has delivered another rate increase, taking its cash rate to **4.85%**, while markets increasingly debate whether the RBA is near the end of its cycle. Japan is seeing expectations of further BOJ tightening, though the pace remains more limited than elsewhere.

The important distinction is that these central banks are not starting from the same position. Some have already tightened considerably. Others are still expected to move gradually. And some have less room to raise rates without putting additional pressure on growth. That creates **policy divergence** — even if several banks raise rates, the market still has to decide which one is likely to deliver *more* tightening than investors currently expect.

## Why the highest interest rate doesn't automatically win

Consider the Australian dollar. The RBA's latest increase takes its cash rate to 4.85%, the highest since 2008. On the surface, that looks supportive for AUD. Higher rates can make a currency more attractive by improving its yield advantage — but traders don't respond only to the current policy rate. They respond to what they expect the bank to do *next*.

If the RBA has already delivered several consecutive hikes, investors may start asking whether the cycle is close to its peak. That changes the calculation. Imagine two central banks:

- **Central Bank A** has a policy rate of 4.85%, but investors increasingly believe its next move is a pause.
- **Central Bank B** has a lower policy rate, but investors expect several more increases.

Central Bank A currently offers the higher rate. Central Bank B may offer the more attractive expected *path*. That doesn't guarantee B's currency outperforms — growth, inflation, risk sentiment and positioning still matter — but it explains why a currency can struggle even after its central bank raises rates.

:::quote
A rate hike is not automatically bullish. The market cares about how the decision changes expectations.
:::

## The dollar has more than one source of support

The US dollar has an important advantage in the current environment. The market picture points to a more hawkish Fed, with December rate-hike expectations around **88%** and US Treasury yields around **5.13–5.14%**. Higher US yields can support the dollar by increasing the relative attractiveness of US assets. But the dollar may *also* benefit from geopolitical uncertainty: when investors worry about global risks, they often increase demand for liquid assets and currencies, and the dollar's role in global finance reinforces that demand.

These two forces can work in the same direction:

- **Higher expected US interest rates** support the yield argument.
- **Geopolitical uncertainty** supports the demand-for-liquidity argument.

However, they are *separate* drivers. A dollar rally during an oil shock does not necessarily mean the US economy is benefiting from higher energy prices — it may simply mean investors currently prefer dollar liquidity and US yields relative to the alternatives. That distinction matters: if tension eases but US rate expectations stay high, the dollar may retain some support; if both forces reverse, it could lose two sources of demand at once.

## When higher rates become a problem for the economy

There is a limit to how much tightening an economy can absorb. Higher oil prices can increase inflation *while* reducing the money households have to spend elsewhere. If central banks respond with further rate increases, borrowing costs rise too. That creates a difficult combination:

- Businesses face higher energy and financing costs.
- Households face higher living expenses and borrowing costs.
- Consumer demand may weaken.
- Growth can slow even while inflation stays elevated.

This is why an oil-driven rate hike is not necessarily as positive for a currency as a hike caused by strong growth. A bank raising rates because demand is robust may be signalling a relatively healthy economy. A bank raising rates because energy prices are pushing inflation higher may be dealing with a far less comfortable situation. The policy action looks similar; the conditions behind it are different — and for FX traders, that distinction is important.

## What the latest policy picture tells us

The market snapshot for 15 November shows several different policy paths. These are market *expectations*, not promises — they can change quickly as new inflation, employment and growth data arrive.

Fed | ~88% chance of a December hike | Whether inflation and yields keep pushing expected US rates higher
ECB | ~20 bp of tightening by year-end | Whether energy inflation outweighs modest growth
BoE | ~21 bp by year-end | Whether inflation persistence can justify tightening if growth weakens
RBA | Cash rate at 4.85% after another hike | Whether the market is approaching the end of the hiking cycle

The table shows why simply ranking currencies by their current interest rates would miss the bigger picture. The market is constantly comparing what each central bank is expected to do *next*.

## What FX traders should watch next

The next stage depends on whether the supply disruption persists and how central banks respond. Five things are worth monitoring:

- **Oil prices and the duration of the shock.** A short-lived spike may have limited effect on inflation expectations; a prolonged period of higher energy prices is more likely to influence business costs, consumer prices and central-bank decisions.
- **Relative interest-rate expectations.** A central bank becoming more hawkish isn't enough on its own — the question is whether it becomes more hawkish *relative to its peers*.
- **Inflation versus growth.** If inflation keeps rising while growth weakens, central banks face a harder trade-off, and the currency response becomes less straightforward than a simple higher-rates narrative suggests.
- **Energy exposure and external balances.** Importers may face pressure on their trade balances while exporters receive a revenue boost — but the size of the effect depends on each country's actual exposure.
- **What currencies are doing relative to one another.** Is dollar strength broad-based? Are energy-sensitive currencies underperforming? Is the yen weakening despite higher intervention risk? Are currencies with similar rate expectations behaving differently? Price action tests whether the macro explanation is actually showing up in the market.

## The FXStrength perspective

An oil shock does not create a universal currency reaction. It changes the economic pressures facing different countries; those pressures influence inflation, growth and central-bank decisions; and investors then compare the expected policy and economic paths across currencies. That is why the same oil shock can support one currency, pressure another and leave a third relatively unchanged.

The key is to avoid treating a rate hike, an inflation surprise or an oil-price increase as a standalone trading signal. Instead, ask:

- Which economy is most exposed?
- Which central bank has the least room to respond?
- Which policy path is changing relative to expectations?
- Is the currency market confirming that interpretation?

## Key takeaways

- Higher oil prices affect currencies through trade balances, inflation and growth.
- Energy exporters and importers can experience very different economic effects.
- Central banks may all face inflation pressure, but their ability and willingness to tighten differ.
- The highest current interest rate does not automatically produce the strongest currency.
- Relative changes in expected rates matter more than simply counting rate hikes.
- A prolonged oil shock can create a difficult combination of higher inflation and weaker growth.

**The question for traders is not just who is raising rates. It is which economy can afford to keep raising them — and whether the market has already priced that in.**
