When conflict threatens energy supplies, oil prices can rise sharply. It's tempting to expect gold to rally at the same time: both are widely watched during geopolitical stress, and gold is often treated as a safe haven. But the two markets don't respond to exactly the same forces.

Oil is a physical commodity — its price is heavily influenced by supply, demand, inventories, transport routes and refining capacity. Gold has a different set of drivers: real interest rates, the US dollar, investor demand and expectations for monetary policy. That distinction matters when geopolitical risk pushes oil higher while US yields and the dollar stay firm.

In the recent setup, Brent crude moved above **$100 a barrel** as Middle East disruptions threatened energy shipments and refining capacity. Gold, meanwhile, faced pressure from a strong dollar and elevated Treasury yields before subsequently rebounding.

The lesson isn't that gold failed as a safe haven. It's that **a geopolitical shock can support one asset while the forces acting on another pull in the opposite direction.**

## Why oil can keep rising

Oil prices respond to the balance between available supply and expected demand. If conflict disrupts shipments, threatens a major shipping route or damages refinery capacity, traders may price in a tighter supply outlook — and prices can rise even before the full physical impact appears in official data.

Duration matters. A brief disruption may create a temporary premium; a prolonged one can affect inventories, transport costs, fuel availability and business expenses. So oil can rise because the market expects fewer barrels or refined products relative to demand — not simply because investors feel nervous.

That distinction also explains why diplomatic headlines don't always reverse the move immediately. A headline may reduce one perceived risk without restoring disrupted supply or repairing damaged infrastructure.

## Why gold can respond differently

Gold doesn't have the same direct supply-demand link to energy transport. Its price is pulled by several forces that can conflict with one another:

- **The US dollar.** Gold is commonly quoted in dollars, so a stronger dollar can make it more expensive for buyers using other currencies and weigh on its price, all else equal.
- **Real yields.** Gold pays no interest. When inflation-adjusted yields rise, interest-bearing assets become relatively more attractive — a headwind for gold, though the relationship isn't mechanical.
- **Interest-rate expectations.** If an energy shock raises inflation concerns and markets expect central banks to keep rates higher for longer, that can support yields and the dollar — both working against gold even while uncertainty is elevated.
- **Safe-haven demand.** Investors may still buy gold during stress, but that demand competes with the forces above. The net result depends on which dominate over the period examined.

This is why *"geopolitical risk is rising"* is not, by itself, a complete explanation for gold's next move.

## The inflation connection makes it more complicated

An oil shock creates a difficult combination for central banks. Higher energy costs push up headline inflation and can squeeze household purchasing power; if the shock persists, businesses may pass more costs on. Central banks then have to judge whether inflation will fade naturally or spread into domestic prices and wages.

Markets may respond by raising expected interest rates or delaying expected cuts. If that repricing lifts yields and supports the dollar, gold can face pressure at the same time oil is rising. But that outcome isn't guaranteed — if investors become more worried about growth, financial instability or policy credibility, yields or the dollar may move differently, and gold can find more support.

The important point: the oil shock doesn't dictate one inevitable gold response. **It changes the conditions under which several markets are being priced.**

## What should traders watch?

Rather than treating oil and gold as a single "geopolitical risk" trade, examine the drivers separately:

- **Oil** — are physical shipments still disrupted? Are inventories, refining capacity or transport routes showing signs of tighter supply?
- **US dollar** — is USD strengthening broadly, or is the move concentrated in a few pairs?
- **Treasury yields** — are nominal yields rising? Where possible, compare them with *real* yields to better gauge the pressure on gold.
- **Rate expectations** — is the market pricing a more restrictive central-bank path because of persistent inflation risk?
- **Gold's response** — is gold holding up despite higher yields and a stronger dollar, or struggling to respond to safe-haven demand?

No single indicator proves the explanation. The value comes from seeing whether several pieces of evidence support the same interpretation.

## What would change the view?

If energy disruptions ease and oil retreats, some inflation pressure may fade; if yields and the dollar also soften, gold could find support even as the risk premium in oil declines. Conversely, if supply stays constrained while real yields and the dollar rise, gold could struggle despite continued uncertainty. A renewed gold rally *alongside* rising yields would be important evidence too — it could suggest safe-haven or other buying is outweighing the usual yield headwind.

The task is to observe that change, not assume it in advance.

## The lesson

Oil prices the availability of energy. Gold prices a different mix of monetary conditions, currency effects and investor demand. Both can react to the same headline without moving in the same direction. For FX traders, following the dollar and yields alongside commodities helps distinguish a physical supply shock from the wider financial-market response.

:::quote
Price is evidence, not proof. The explanation becomes more convincing when the behaviour of related markets supports it.
