Switzerland's policy rate is 0%. US interest rates are considerably higher — and even after Friday's weak US jobs report, the US 10-year Treasury yield remained around 5.2%. Yet the Swiss franc has been strengthening sharply.

So why are investors buying a currency that pays almost nothing when higher-yielding alternatives are available? **Because sometimes, yield isn't what the market is buying.**

The recent CHF move brings together several forces at once: geopolitical uncertainty, global bond-market stress, European fiscal concerns, changing US rate expectations and potentially the unwinding of CHF-funded carry trades. Friday's weak US jobs report added another piece — but the reaction in Treasury yields shows why the story isn't as simple as *weak data = lower yields = weaker dollar.*

## What Happened?

The franc has strengthened sharply against both the US dollar and the euro over the past several sessions. But the move did not begin with Friday's Nonfarm Payrolls report — CHF was already benefiting as global bond markets came under pressure and European fiscal concerns intensified. The euro in particular came under pressure against the franc as the spread between French and German 10-year yields widened to its highest level since around 2012.

Then came the US jobs report. The economy added only **29,000 jobs** in September, versus expectations of around 90,000. Previous months were revised lower, unemployment rose from 4.1% to 4.2%, and average hourly earnings increased just 0.1% month-on-month, down from 0.3%. That was a substantial downside surprise. Markets sharply reduced expectations for another Fed hike in October, and the dollar weakened.

But there is an important wrinkle: **Treasury yields initially fell — and then moved back up.** The 10-year had been around 5.34% before the report; it declined after the release, then reversed higher. So the NFP story is *not* simply *weak jobs → lower yields → weaker USD → stronger CHF.* The reality is more complicated.

## Why Is the Swiss Franc Strengthening?

There are several overlapping explanations.

### 1. Geopolitical risk is creating safe-haven demand

The ongoing conflict involving the US and Iran, together with wider Middle East instability, has increased uncertainty across markets. That matters for CHF because the franc has an established safe-haven role — the Swiss National Bank itself has noted the franc is sought after as a safe haven during uncertainty, and that the Middle East escalation has increased upward pressure on it.

This doesn't mean CHF automatically rises every time geopolitical risk increases. But when investors become more defensive, the characteristics associated with Switzerland — political and institutional stability, relatively strong public finances, a long history of attracting defensive capital — can become more valuable. And this stress is arriving at the same time as a major bond-market shock.

### 2. Bond-market stress is part of the story

Global government bond yields have risen sharply. The US 10-year reached around 5.34%, its highest in roughly 24 years, while French yields also hit multi-decade highs. This isn't simply a US story — European bond markets have been under pressure too, particularly France. The French–German 10-year spread widened beyond **140 basis points**, levels last seen around the European debt crisis.

If investors become less comfortable with European sovereign risk, demand for euros can weaken. If they simultaneously become more defensive, demand for CHF can increase. That creates a potentially powerful combination:

:::flow
European risk rises → EUR becomes less attractive → defensive demand increases → CHF benefits
:::

The franc doesn't necessarily need a new positive economic story. The alternatives can simply become less attractive.

### 3. The NFP shock weakened one of the dollar's supports

Friday's report still matters — just not in the simplistic way it first appears. The 29K gain was far below expectations, unemployment rose, wage growth slowed and prior months were revised lower, substantially reducing October Fed-hike expectations. That removes some support from the dollar.

But look at the yield reaction. The initial move was exactly what you'd expect from a weak labour report — *NFP disappoints → hike expectations fall → yields initially decline* — but the decline didn't last. Yields moved back higher. Why? Because the long end of the Treasury market is influenced by much more than the next Fed meeting: inflation expectations, energy prices, fiscal concerns, Treasury supply, term premium and the broader competition for capital.

So the report could be dovish for the *expected path of Fed policy* without solving the underlying reasons for elevated long-term yields. The weak jobs report removed some support from USD; it did not suddenly turn the entire US bond market into a bullish story. And that distinction matters for CHF.

### 4. The carry trade may also be unwinding

The franc is a low-yielding currency and can be used to fund carry trades:

:::flow
Borrow cheaply in CHF → invest in higher-yielding assets → earn the yield difference
:::

That works best when markets are calm. When volatility rises sharply, leveraged positions become less attractive, investors reduce them, and they buy back the currency they borrowed. If CHF was the funding currency, that creates additional CHF demand:

:::flow
Volatility rises → carry positions reduced → CHF bought back → CHF strengthens
:::

There are indications the recent environment has been conducive to such an unwind, and the sharp EUR/CHF move has been described as unwinding a crowded carry trade. But this is an important distinction: a carry-trade unwind is a *plausible* explanation, not something we can prove from the price chart alone. Treat it as part of the story, not the story.

### 5. Europe is adding another layer

The euro has its own problems. French fiscal concerns have intensified, widening the French–German 10-year spread — effectively a market measure of how much extra yield investors demand to hold French debt over German. A wider spread doesn't automatically mean France is heading for a crisis, but it does say investors are demanding a larger risk premium. At the same time, geopolitical tension and higher energy costs complicate the European inflation and growth outlook. It's not *"Switzerland is booming"* — it's *"Europe looks more complicated, while CHF is treated as a defensive alternative."*

## What About the SNB?

The Swiss National Bank left its policy rate at **0%** on September 24, and explicitly said it was willing to intervene in the FX market if necessary. Its own forecast assumes the rate stays at 0% through the forecast horizon. Swiss inflation was just 0.8% in August, though the SNB noted higher energy prices had pushed it up and that the Middle East situation created significant uncertainty.

So this is *not* a conventional hawkish-central-bank story. The SNB isn't raising rates and making CHF attractive through a growing yield advantage — in fact it has acknowledged the risk that excessive CHF appreciation could become a problem and retained the ability to intervene. That's what makes the move interesting: **CHF is strengthening despite its low interest rate.**

## The Interesting Part: Investors May Be Buying Safety, Not Yield

This is probably the most important lesson in the move. Under normal conditions, a higher-rate currency can attract capital because investors are compensated for holding it. But currencies aren't bought exclusively for yield. Investors can also care about:

- safety
- liquidity
- political stability
- fiscal credibility
- diversification
- positioning
- volatility
- relative risk

During calm markets, carry can dominate. During stressed markets, capital preservation can become more important than carry. That's the environment in which a 0% currency can outperform a much higher-yielding one.

## What Price Is Saying

We don't need to claim every CHF move is caused by one factor. Instead, look for confirmation across markets. Right now, several pieces point the same general direction:

- CHF ↑
- EUR/CHF ↓
- European sovereign stress ↑
- Geopolitical uncertainty ↑
- US October Fed-hike expectations ↓ after weak NFP
- USD ↓ after the jobs report
- US Treasury yields ↓ initially, then ↑ again

That last line is particularly important. The Treasury chart tells us the NFP reaction wasn't a clean bond-market repricing — the dollar-side story and the long-end bond story *diverged.* That is exactly why looking at several markets together is more useful than relying on one headline.

## Why Does This Matter for FX Traders?

Because FX is a relative market. When USD/CHF falls, it's tempting to say *"the Swiss franc is strong."* But that's only half the question. You also need to ask: is CHF strengthening, is USD weakening, or both? And then: what does EUR/CHF say?

If CHF is strengthening against *both* USD and EUR, that's stronger evidence of a broad CHF story rather than a USD-specific move. That's where a currency-strength framework helps: instead of analysing one pair in isolation, look across multiple CHF crosses and timeframes. If CHF strength is broad-based, the safe-haven read gains support. If it appears only against USD while EUR/CHF is stable, the explanation may be far more dollar-specific. The cross-market evidence helps separate the story from the headline.

## What Could Keep CHF Strong?

- **Geopolitical risk.** Further Middle East escalation could increase demand for defensive assets.
- **European fiscal stress.** If concerns around France or wider European sovereign risk intensify, CHF could keep benefiting relative to EUR.
- **Further US economic weakness.** More soft US data could reduce Fed-tightening expectations further, removing another dollar support.
- **Continued volatility.** Elevated bond-market volatility keeps leveraged positions and carry trades under pressure.

None of these is guaranteed — the market can change its mind quickly.

## What Could Reverse the Move?

The same forces can work in reverse. If geopolitical tensions ease, safe-haven demand could fade. If European fiscal concerns stabilise, pressure on EUR could disappear. If US data strengthens and inflation stays sticky, Fed-tightening expectations could return. And if global volatility falls, carry trades could become attractive again.

The point isn't to predict which scenario happens next — it's to identify **which variable would invalidate the current explanation.** That's a far more useful way to trade a macro story.

## The Bigger Lesson

The Swiss franc is a good reminder that currencies don't always strengthen because their domestic economy suddenly became more attractive. Sometimes a currency rises because the rest of the market suddenly looks less comfortable. Right now, CHF sits at the intersection of several forces:

- geopolitical risk
- bond-market stress
- European fiscal concerns
- changing US rate expectations
- potential carry-trade unwinding

Friday's weak NFP added another reason to reduce some dollar exposure. But the Treasury yield reversal tells us not to oversimplify: the report weakened the case for an immediate October Fed hike, but it did not make the broader US long-end bond problem disappear. And that's precisely what makes the move interesting.

## The FXStrength Takeaway

When a currency moves sharply, don't immediately ask *"what happened in that country?"* Ask: *"what changed about the relative attractiveness of owning this currency versus the alternatives?"*

For CHF right now, the evidence points to something broader than a simple interest-rate story. Switzerland isn't suddenly offering a superior yield. Instead, CHF is benefiting from what investors want when uncertainty rises: perceived stability, liquidity and a place to reduce risk. Friday's NFP shock added a second force by weakening one of the dollar's supports — even though the subsequent rise in Treasury yields showed the bond-market story remains much more complicated.

:::quote
A currency can strengthen not because its yield became more attractive, but because the market suddenly values what that currency represents more than the yield it pays.
:::

## What to Watch Next

The CHF story becomes more convincing if CHF keeps strengthening broadly across major crosses; European sovereign spreads stay elevated; US data keeps weakening *without* a collapse in long-term yields; geopolitical risk stays high; and evidence of carry-trade unwinding persists. It becomes less convincing if those conditions reverse.

:::quote
Price is evidence, not proof.
:::

The job isn't to find one explanation for the move. It's to keep testing the explanation against what the other markets are saying.
