Gold's latest sell-off has put traders in a difficult position. After reaching record highs earlier this year, the metal has suffered a substantial correction, with Friday's close near **$3,875**.

The immediate pressures are familiar: a hawkish Federal Reserve, rising Treasury yields, and investors selling liquid assets during the technology-led equity sell-off. But there's a bigger question than whether gold is technically oversold: **who is still buying gold, who is selling, and at what price might those two forces finally meet?** To answer that, we need to look beyond the futures chart.

## China: a major source of demand, but not an unlimited price floor

China has been one of the most important parts of this year's gold story. According to Heraeus data reported by Kitco, China imported **142 tonnes** in August, bringing imports in the first eight months of 2026 to **1,141 tonnes** — already above the 940 tonnes imported in all of 2025. The increase was driven partly by investment demand, even as high prices weighed on jewellery consumption.

The People's Bank of China has also continued adding to official reserves. The World Gold Council reported the PBoC bought **23 tonnes in September**, taking its third-quarter purchases to 63 tonnes. These figures suggest demand hasn't been driven solely by short-term Western investment flows.

There's an important qualification, though. The latest Reuters reporting indicates physical buying in China and India had become *subdued* during the recent price decline. Lower prices may eventually attract buyers, but there's no guarantee they step in immediately. **China is an important source of demand — not a guaranteed buyer at every price.**

*Sources: [Kitco / Heraeus](https://www.kitco.com/news/article/2026-09-28/china-pace-import-1700-tonnes-gold-2026-silver-price-faces-key-test-near) · [World Gold Council — China Q3 update](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/gold-focus/2026/10/china-gold-market-update-q3-ends-strong-official-gold-buying).*

## Central banks: who holds the gold?

Central-bank holdings add another dimension. The United States holds approximately **8,133 tonnes** of official reserves; Germany around 3,349; Italy 2,452; France 2,437; and China's reported holdings were about 2,410 tonnes in September. These are substantial stocks — but they need to be interpreted correctly.

- **Large established holders** (the US, major European holders) sitting on big reserves does **not** mean they're actively buying large quantities every month.
- **China, by contrast,** has been adding to its reported reserves — and other emerging-market central banks have been important buyers in recent years.

The distinction matters: the *size* of a country's reserves tells us who already owns gold; *changes* in those reserves tell us who is adding or reducing exposure. (The European figures need care too — Germany, France and Italy hold their own national reserves; there's no single pooled EU gold reserve replacing them.)

*Source: [World Gold Council — Gold Reserves by Country](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/data/gold-reserves-by-country).*

## The financial market can still overwhelm physical demand

If central banks and some Asian investors keep accumulating, why can the price fall so sharply? Because the international gold price is also heavily influenced by **financial-market positioning.** ETF investors can reduce exposure, futures traders can close leveraged positions, and funds facing losses elsewhere can sell liquid assets to raise cash.

That last point is especially relevant during an equity sell-off. Gold may be held as portfolio protection, but when investors need liquidity it can become something they *sell.* The recent technology-led liquidation may have accelerated that — though the available evidence doesn't establish how much of Friday's decline came directly from margin calls.

There's also the interest-rate problem. The Fed's hawkish hold — and two dissents favouring an immediate hike — strengthened expectations of further tightening. With the US 10-year yield around **5.15%**, holding non-yielding gold became more expensive relative to interest-bearing assets. So gold was pressured from both sides: higher yields made it less attractive, while financial liquidation created additional selling.

## Where could gold finally find support?

The first area to watch is **$3,875–$3,850**, around Friday's low and the next immediate technical test on the supplied market map. If buyers defend it and price begins to stabilise, it would suggest some demand is absorbing the selling. But a brief bounce isn't enough to establish a floor — a sustained recovery would be more convincing alongside stabilising ETF flows, stronger physical premiums, or evidence that central-bank buying remains firm.

If $3,850 gives way decisively, the next area identified on the technical map is **$3,780–$3,800.** That wouldn't automatically make gold a bargain; traders would need to reassess whether the decline is simply extending a financial-market correction or whether the underlying demand picture is deteriorating. The levels are areas to monitor, not guaranteed turning points.

## What would tell us the selling is running out?

Three developments deserve attention:

- **Physical demand** — do Chinese and Indian buyers return as prices fall, and do local premiums improve?
- **Investment flows** — do gold ETF outflows and futures selling begin to moderate?
- **Rates and the dollar** — do Treasury yields stay elevated, or does a change in rate expectations remove some of gold's opportunity-cost pressure?

The combination matters more than any single indicator. If physical and official-sector demand stay firm while financial selling fades, the market could begin to stabilise. If Asian buying stays weak, ETF outflows continue and yields stay elevated, gold may need to search for a lower level.

## The lesson

:::quote
Gold's long-term demand story and its short-term price action can point in different directions.
:::

China's imports and central-bank accumulation show structural demand remains relevant. But large official reserves don't guarantee fresh buying, and physical demand can't always absorb rapid financial selling. For traders, the question isn't simply whether gold has fallen far enough — it's where real buying demand begins to absorb the supply, and whether the evidence supports a floor rather than just another temporary bounce.
