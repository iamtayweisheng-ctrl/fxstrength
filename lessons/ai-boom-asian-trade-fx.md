The artificial-intelligence boom is often discussed through chipmakers, technology stocks and data-centre investment. But it's also showing up in the movement of goods across borders.

Taiwan's exports reached a record **US$87.22 billion in September 2026**, rising 60.9% from a year earlier — beating expectations and marking the 35th consecutive month of year-on-year growth, with strong demand for AI chips and technology products a major factor. China's September exports rose **36.3% year on year**, their fastest growth since February; semiconductor exports rose sharply, while autos and green-technology products also contributed.

These figures point to strong external demand for parts of Asia's technology and manufacturing supply chains. They don't, on their own, tell us what either currency should do next.

*Sources: [Reuters — Taiwan exports hit a record](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/taiwan-september-exports-hit-fresh-monthly-record-ai-demand-us-leads-2026-10-08/); [Reuters — China's September exports surge](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinas-exports-surge-363-yy-september-imports-up-31-2026-10-14/).*

## Why stronger exports don't guarantee currency strength

A stronger export sector can support a currency — overseas customers need to pay for goods, and sustained export growth can improve a country's trade balance and outlook. But the relationship isn't automatic.

- **Exports are only one part of the external balance.** Imports can rise alongside exports. In Taiwan, September imports also grew strongly, by 51.7% year on year. The overall trade balance depends on both sides, not export growth in isolation.
- **Trade flows aren't the same as capital flows.** A country can generate substantial export revenue while investors move money elsewhere. Foreign investment, portfolio flows, hedging and demand for overseas assets all affect currency demand.
- **The central bank's framework matters.** Authorities may prefer to limit exchange-rate volatility rather than let the currency strengthen freely, so the pace of appreciation can differ from the pace of export growth.
- **Markets look forward.** If investors already expect strong technology exports, a fresh increase in shipments may matter less than a result that materially exceeds expectations — or evidence the boom is weakening.

## Taiwan and China are not the same FX story

Both economies benefit from global technology demand, but their currency dynamics shouldn't be treated as interchangeable.

Taiwan's export surge reflects its role in the global technology supply chain, including demand for electronic components and information products; the United States was its largest export destination in September. China's export growth was broader — semiconductors, automobiles and green-technology products — and its overall trade surplus widened, while domestic demand and the property sector remained important constraints on the wider outlook.

For the **New Taiwan dollar** and **Chinese yuan**, the relevant questions differ. Export performance matters, but so do each economy's capital flows, policy settings, trade relationships and exchange-rate management. A large trade surplus is evidence of strong net goods exports — it isn't proof that a currency must appreciate.

## What should FX traders watch?

**Is export growth broadening or concentrated?** If growth relies heavily on a narrow group of AI-related products, the currency implication may differ from a broad-based improvement across manufacturing and services.

**Are imports and the trade balance moving in the same direction?** Rapid export growth can coexist with rapid import growth. The overall balance provides more context than exports alone.

**Is the currency confirming the economic story?** Compare the trade data with the currency's actual behaviour, broader regional FX performance and policy signals. If exports accelerate but the currency doesn't strengthen, that isn't necessarily a market mistake — other forces may be outweighing the trade channel.

**Are markets revising expectations?** A record export number is backward-looking. Guidance, new orders, semiconductor demand and revisions to future growth expectations may matter more for the next stage of pricing.

## What would change the view?

If export growth broadens, the trade balance improves and capital flows reinforce the trend, the case for currency support becomes stronger. If growth is concentrated, already priced in, or offset by other outflows and policy choices, the FX response may be muted. A sustained slowdown in AI-related orders would also challenge the idea that the current export surge will continue.

## The lesson

:::quote
Trade data tells you where goods are moving. It does not tell you the whole story of where money is moving.
:::

AI demand is an important force in Asia's export outlook, but FX traders need to connect exports with the external balance, capital flows, policy and price confirmation. The question isn't simply *"which country exports more?"* It's *"what does this change in the economic outlook — and is the currency responding to it?"*
