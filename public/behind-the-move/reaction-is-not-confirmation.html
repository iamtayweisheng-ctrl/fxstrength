The Fed Didn't Hike. So Why Did the Dollar Rally?

Markets don't need a rate change to reprice a currency — they only need their expectations to change. But a reaction is not the same as confirmation.

5 Sep 2026 · 8 min read

Jackson Hole arrived this week with no rate decision, no new dot plot, and no immediate policy change.

The Federal Funds target remained 3.50–3.75%, exactly where it was before the speeches began.

And yet the US Dollar rallied. Treasury yields rose. Rate expectations shifted.

If you were watching the headlines, you might have been confused. How does a currency strengthen when its central bank does nothing?

The answer is that central banks can move markets without changing today's interest rate. They can change what the market expects about tomorrow's interest rate. This week, that is what happened.

But there is an important second part to the story. The Dollar reacted strongly. It has not yet fully confirmed the move.

1. The Surprise Wasn't a Rate Hike

Let's be clear about what did not happen.

There was no 25-basis-point increase. There was no emergency tightening. The Federal Reserve did not change its policy rate. The event was Warsh's speech.

For a beginner, this can feel strange. We are trained to think markets move when central banks act. But in foreign exchange, markets also move when central banks change expectations about what they might do next.

The market entered Jackson Hole with a reasonably strong assumption that the Fed was already restrictive and that the longer-term discussion would eventually turn toward easing.

Warsh challenged that assumption. His message was effectively:

Don't assume the Fed is finished with tightening.

That is not a rate hike. But it changes the range of possible future outcomes. And in FX, that can be enough to move the Dollar.

2. The Surprise Was the Message

The important part of the speech was not simply that Warsh sounded hawkish. It was that he challenged an idea the market had already started to accept: that current policy was restrictive enough.

Warsh pointed to resilient economic activity and strong private capital expenditure — particularly around AI infrastructure — and argued that recent inflation progress had not meaningfully solved the underlying problem.

In other words, he pushed the market to consider a less dovish future. The expected policy path shifted. That is what matters.

📌 Key Insight

The market does not need to believe that a hike is definitely coming. It only needs to assign a higher probability to tighter policy than it did before.

3. Markets Repriced the Future

When a central banker challenges the market's baseline, the first thing to watch is the expected policy path. Markets moved toward:

  • Less confidence in near-term rate cuts.
  • Greater probability of tighter policy later in 2026.
  • Higher expected rates for longer.

You cannot see an expectation directly on a chart. But you can see how the market expresses that expectation. One of the clearest places to look is the Treasury market.

4. Bonds Confirmed the Initial Message

Following Warsh's speech, Treasury yields moved higher, particularly at the short end.

The 2-year Treasury yield is especially useful here because it is closely connected to expectations for the Fed's policy path over the next 12–24 months.

The sequence was:

Warsh's speech→rate expectations shift→2Y yields rise→US rate advantage increases→USD rallies

That is much more useful than simply saying "Warsh spoke → USD went up." It gives us the transmission mechanism. And it gives us something else: a way to check whether the market actually believed the message.

5. But Did the Dollar Actually Confirm It?

This is where the story becomes more interesting. The Dollar's immediate reaction was clear.

DXY jumped after the jobs report later reinforced the hawkish repricing — but the broader chart still shows an important limitation: the Dollar remains below the 99.42 key level. It has not yet reclaimed the higher 100.50 level either.

So we should distinguish between two things:

  • Reaction: the Dollar moved higher as rate expectations became more hawkish.
  • Confirmation: the Dollar would need to sustain that move and break important levels before we could say the market had fully accepted a new bullish regime.

That distinction matters. A large move is not automatically a regime change.

Price is evidence, not proof.

6. The Political Constraint

There is also another force operating in the background. The Trump administration has repeatedly called for lower interest rates. That creates an interesting tension for the market.

On one side: sticky inflation, Warsh's message and strong employment data push toward higher-rate expectations. On the other: political pressure for lower rates creates uncertainty about how far a hawkish repricing can ultimately go.

We should not assume that political pressure explains every failed breakout in DXY — we cannot establish that from the chart alone. But it is part of the broader environment in which today's rate expectations are being formed. And this is exactly why a single speech should not be treated as the whole story.

7. Then Came the Jobs Report

The August employment report provided another important piece of evidence.

Markets had expected only 56,000 new jobs. The actual figure was 162,000 — a substantial upside surprise. More importantly, previous months were revised higher by a combined 55,000, further weakening the idea that the US labour market was rapidly deteriorating.

This mattered because it supported the hawkish interpretation already introduced at Jackson Hole. The sequence became:

Hawkish Fed message→higher rate expectations→strong jobs data→expectations reinforced→USD strengthens

But even here, the same principle applies. The data can reinforce a narrative without guaranteeing that the Dollar will continue higher indefinitely.

8. The Real Lesson

Putting the pieces together, we get a simple framework:

Central bank message→expected policy path→bond market reaction→currency reaction→price confirmation

Each step tells us something different. The speech tells us what the central bank is communicating. Rates tell us how the market is repricing the future. FX tells us how that repricing is being expressed in currency prices. And the chart tells us whether the move is actually developing into something larger.

This week, the first three steps were clear. The question is whether the fourth step becomes sustained confirmation.

What to Remember

A central bank does not need to change rates to change a currency. But a currency reaction is not the same as a confirmed trend.

  • A speech can change expectations without changing today's policy rate.
  • Treasury yields help show whether those expectations are actually being repriced.
  • A strong economic report can reinforce a hawkish narrative.
  • But price still needs to confirm whether that repricing is becoming a sustained market regime.
  • Political and economic constraints can challenge expectations later.

Question of the Week

🧭 Question of the Week

A central banker gives a hawkish speech, Treasury yields rise, and the Dollar jumps — but DXY remains below an important resistance level. What should you conclude?

  • A. The Dollar must continue higher.
  • B. The market reacted to the hawkish message, but the broader price confirmation is still incomplete.
  • C. The Treasury market is wrong.

The reaction tells us that expectations changed. The fact that price remains below an important level tells us that the broader market structure has not necessarily changed yet. That is the difference between observing a move and assuming a trend.

Answer: B

The Lesson

Expectations can change before the trend does.

Jackson Hole showed how a central bank can move currencies without touching interest rates. Warsh changed the market's perception of the possible policy path. Treasury yields responded. The Dollar responded. Then the strong August jobs report reinforced that repricing.

But the Dollar has still not broken through the 99.42 key level on the chart. That is the part worth remembering. A market can react to a story before it fully believes the story.

The job of the trader is not to predict that the first reaction must become a trend. It is to observe whether the evidence continues to support it.

What You Learned

A central bank can move a currency by changing expectations, not just rates. But a reaction to a hawkish message is not yet a confirmed regime — the move still has to hold and break the levels that define it.

What Price Is Saying

DXY reacted, but it remains below the 99.42 key level. Price is evidence, not proof: the first move tells you the market noticed; a sustained break tells you the market has repriced. Until then, "reaction" and "confirmation" are two different things.
