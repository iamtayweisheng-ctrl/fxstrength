The Same Oil Shock, Two Very Different Central Banks

Why persistently high energy prices may put the Bank of Canada under more pressure to tighten than the Bank of England.

18 Oct 2026 · 4 min read

Oil prices have surged as conflict in the Middle East disrupts shipments and damages refining capacity. For central banks, that creates an awkward question: should they look through higher energy prices, or worry the shock will spread into the wider economy? The Bank of Canada and the Bank of England are confronting that question from different starting points.

On October 17, Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem said the Bank expected to raise its quarterly inflation forecast significantly because crude oil and petroleum-product prices had remained high. He warned that the risk of inflation becoming broader and more persistent had increased.

Across the Atlantic, Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey has cautioned against drawing conclusions too early. So far, evidence of widespread second-round price increases remains limited — though he has warned that sustained energy costs could eventually force businesses to raise prices.

The difference isn't that one central bank cares about inflation and the other doesn't. It's how much evidence each sees that an energy shock is becoming a lasting domestic inflation problem.

Sources: Reuters — Bank of Canada inflation outlook; Financial Times — Bailey on energy prices.

Why oil doesn't automatically mean rate hikes

Higher oil prices can push headline inflation up quickly. But central banks must decide whether that increase will fade when energy prices stabilise, or spread into wages, services and businesses' pricing decisions.

That distinction matters because monetary policy can't produce more oil or repair disrupted refineries. Raising rates can, however, weaken demand and help prevent a temporary price shock from becoming entrenched inflation.

The Bank of Canada is signalling that the risk of broader inflation has increased; Macklem said the Bank would assess whether inflation was likely to return towards its 2% target on its own or whether policy would need to be adjusted. The Bank of England is alert to the same risk, but Bailey has pointed to limited evidence so far that higher energy costs are already producing widespread second-round effects.

Neither position guarantees the next rate decision. They show what each central bank is watching.

What this means for CAD and GBP

The FX implication isn't simply "oil up, CAD up" or "inflation up, currency up."

Canada's energy exposure can support parts of its external balance when oil rises, but higher energy costs can also squeeze households and businesses. The net effect on CAD depends on the broader growth outlook, risk sentiment, and how markets interpret the Bank of Canada's likely response.

For GBP, the question is whether higher energy prices begin to change the expected path of Bank of England policy. If markets believe domestic inflation pressures stay contained, a temporary rise in headline inflation may have less lasting impact on rate expectations. If wage and services inflation accelerate, that interpretation could change.

For both currencies, the market reaction depends on what changes relative to expectations — not on the oil price alone.

What to watch next

For CAD:

  • The Bank of Canada's October 28 decision and updated inflation forecast.
  • Whether Macklem's concern about persistent inflation shows up in the policy statement.
  • Evidence that energy costs are spreading into broader prices, wages or inflation expectations.

For GBP:

  • UK inflation and wage data.
  • Whether businesses begin passing energy costs through to customers.
  • Whether Bailey's cautious assessment changes as more evidence arrives.

The important confirmation isn't another high oil-price print. It's evidence that the shock is changing the domestic inflation outlook and the market's expectations for policy.

What would change the view?

If oil retreats and broader inflation pressures stay contained, markets may scale back expectations of tighter policy. If energy prices stay high and second-round effects become clearer, both central banks could face greater pressure to respond — though the timing and degree of that repricing may still differ.

The lesson

Central banks react not only to the shock, but to how the shock travels through their economies. The same oil-price move can create different policy risks in different countries. For FX traders, the useful question isn't simply whether energy prices are rising — it's whether the rise is changing the expected path of interest rates, and whether the currency is already pricing that change.
