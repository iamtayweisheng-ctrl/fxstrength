What Happens After Expectations Change?

Markets can reprice in seconds. The harder question is whether that repricing lasts.

23 Aug 2026 · 11 min read

Last week gave us a near-perfect lesson in why the first move is never the whole story.

On Tuesday, Canadian inflation came in stronger than expected. Markets adjusted: the Bank of Canada might not cut as aggressively, and CAD found support.

By Friday, that expectation was still alive. Then US–Canada trade negotiations collapsed. New tariffs were imposed. And suddenly CAD had a completely different problem.

The inflation story had not become false. It had become secondary.

This is the focus of this week's lesson. Last week we looked at how new information changes expectations. This week we ask: what happens after that change?

Does the new expectation hold? Does the next piece of information validate it, weaken it, or replace it?

The answer is not always in the first move. It is in what happens next.

↳ In short

An expectation change is not the end of the story. The next piece of information can validate it, weaken it, or completely replace it.

1. The First Move Is Only the Opening Act

When a significant data point hits, markets react fast. But that reaction is just the beginning.

What follows is usually more informative:

  • Validation — the move continues, suggesting the market accepts the new expectation.
  • Challenge — the move stalls or reverses, suggesting the market has doubts.
  • Replacement — a new event arrives that changes the dominant story entirely.

All three can follow the same initial surprise. The difference is what happens afterwards.

The first move tells you the market noticed. The second move tells you whether it cares.

2. Canada: A Perfect Example of Replacement

This was the clearest sequence of the week.

Tuesday — the setup. Canadian CPI came in stronger than expected. Markets recalibrated: less room for the Bank of Canada to cut rates, more support for CAD. The expectation changed. CAD strengthened.

Friday — the replacement. US–Canada trade negotiations collapsed. The US imposed new 50% tariffs on roughly $20–28 billion of Canadian goods. Canada responded with dollar-for-dollar retaliation.

Now CAD had two stories:

  • Higher inflation → potentially tighter BoC policy → CAD positive.
  • Trade war → weaker Canadian export outlook → CAD negative.

The inflation data had not been revised away. It simply stopped being the dominant driver.

This is not unusual. It is normal. Markets do not hold every expectation in equal weight forever. When a more urgent story arrives, it can push the previous one into the background.

↳ In short

Canadian CPI gave CAD a reason to strengthen. The trade collapse gave it a bigger reason to weaken. The second story replaced the first.

3. The US Treasury Buyback: When the Repricing Faded

US long-term yields had risen sharply as investors demanded more compensation for holding long-duration debt.

Then the Treasury announced it would at least double certain long-end buyback operations, from $2 billion to $4 billion per operation.

The immediate reaction was clear: long-end yields fell, and the dollar weakened as markets read the move as policymakers becoming more sensitive to rising borrowing costs.

Here the sequence was slightly different from Canada. The new expectation was tested.

Yields later moved back higher. The underlying concerns around inflation, debt and Treasury supply had not disappeared. The market had tried out the idea that policymakers would help cap long-term yields — and then questioned whether that expectation was convincing enough to last.

↳ In short

The Treasury buyback produced an immediate repricing. The rebound in yields showed that the market did not fully accept the new expectation.

4. China: When the Market Hasn't Decided Yet

China's July data provided a different kind of lesson.

Industrial output grew 4.5% year-on-year, down from 5.3% in June and below the 4.8% forecast. Retail sales grew just 0.6%. Fixed-asset investment declined 6.7% in the first seven months of the year.

The message was clear: domestic demand remains weak.

But China is not one story. Industrial production stayed positive, high-tech manufacturing expanded, and exports remained a source of support.

For AUD and NZD, this creates ambiguity. The data created a bearish argument — but price did not immediately validate it.

If China weakness becomes the dominant narrative, those currencies should feel pressure. But if commodity demand and external growth hold up, the market may look past the data. The market may eventually agree with the China-weakness story — but it needs to see that weakness become important enough to dominate the other drivers.

Sometimes the market does not validate or replace the story. It simply waits.

↳ In short

Weak Chinese data created a bearish argument for AUD and NZD. But without clear follow-through, the market is still deciding whether that argument deserves priority.

5. The Euro: What Validation Looks Like

Not every expectation gets challenged. Some get reinforced.

Eurozone August flash PMI showed business activity accelerating to 52.1 — the fastest growth since November and above expectations. Manufacturing was particularly strong, while new orders and export orders improved.

This was not a headline in isolation. The details supported the same story:

  • Stronger manufacturing.
  • Improving new orders.
  • Returning export demand.
  • Improving employment.

When multiple pieces of information point in the same direction, the new expectation becomes easier for the market to accept. There is no competing story yet. There is no reversal. The follow-through confirms the first move.

↳ In short

A repricing becomes more credible when the next available information continues to tell the same story.

6. The Three Paths

Putting the week's examples together, three distinct paths follow an expectation change:

Replacement — Canada

CPI → trade war. A new, more urgent story arrives and takes over.

Questioning — US Treasury

Buyback → yield rebound. The market tests the new expectation and finds it wanting.

Validation — Eurozone

PMI → confirming details. The next information reinforces the same narrative.

Traders often focus only on the initial surprise. But the follow-through is where the real information lives.

↳ In short

After an expectation changes, watch for replacement, questioning or validation. Each tells you something different about what the market actually believes.

7. What Price Is Saying — And What It Isn't

This brings us to the central idea.

Price is evidence, not proof.

If CAD strengthens after Canadian CPI, that tells you the market noticed. If CAD then weakens after the trade collapse, that does not mean the inflation data was wrong — it means another driver became more important.

If USD falls after softer US data but later recovers, that does not mean the data was irrelevant. It may mean the market had already priced much of it. Or another factor offset it. Or traders took profit.

The job is not to explain every tick. It is to determine whether the new narrative is gaining or losing priority.

↳ In short

Price does not prove a macro thesis. But persistent price behaviour can tell you whether the market is treating that thesis as the main story or a side plot.

8. Timeframe Changes the Meaning

A reversal on a five-minute chart and a reversal on a weekly chart are not the same thing.

Imagine USD/CAD falls sharply after Canadian CPI. A day trader may care deeply if the pair immediately reverses. A swing trader may barely notice it. The same price action has different meanings depending on the timeframe.

A short-term reversal may simply be:

  • Profit taking.
  • Positioning adjustments.
  • Liquidity noise.
  • Technical resistance.

A sustained move over several sessions is harder to dismiss. A short-term reversal can be noise; a sustained reversal forces you to reconsider the story.

This matters because the Canada example played out over days. The CPI move on Tuesday and the tariff move on Friday were both visible on hourly and daily charts. A trader watching only the one-minute chart might have missed the actual lesson.

↳ In short

Price only becomes meaningful when you know the timeframe you are trying to explain. The second move often takes time to develop.

9. The Four Questions to Ask After a Market Move

Instead of asking "Where is the currency going?", ask four simpler questions.

  • 1. Did expectations actually change? Was the information significant enough to alter the market's outlook?
  • 2. Did price respond? If not, perhaps the information was already priced in — or another driver is stronger.
  • 3. What happened next? Did the move persist, reverse, or get overtaken by a new story?
  • 4. Did other markets confirm it? Look at yields, commodities, equities and other currencies. If the macro story says USD should weaken but Treasury yields rise and USD holds firm, something deserves further investigation.
↳ In short

Don't stop at the initial reaction. Check whether expectations changed, price responded, the next move confirmed or challenged the story, and other markets agreed.

The Lesson

What happens after expectations change?

The beginner sees:

Data→Price moves

The better trader sees:

Data→Expectations change→Price reacts→Market accepts, questions, or replaces

That final step is where the useful information lives. A surprise can produce a move. What happens next tells you whether that move is being accepted, questioned, or replaced.

Key Takeaways

  • An expectation change is not the end of the story. The first move tells you the market noticed; the second tells you whether the new expectation is actually taking hold.
  • A new story can replace an old one. Canadian CPI gave CAD support. The trade collapse gave it a bigger problem.
  • The market can question an expectation without replacing it. The Treasury buyback produced a repricing that yields later rejected.
  • Sometimes the market simply waits. Chinese data weakened, but AUD and NZD did not necessarily collapse — the market has not yet decided which China story matters most.
  • Validation strengthens credibility. Eurozone PMI was followed by details that pointed the same way.
  • Timeframe matters. The second move often takes days, not minutes.
  • Price is evidence, not proof.

What You Learned

A change in expectations starts the repricing. What happens afterwards tells you whether that new expectation is actually taking hold — or whether it has already been replaced.

What Price Is Saying

The most useful information is often not the first reaction. Watch whether the move persists, reverses, or gets overtaken by a new story. That behaviour tells you whether the market is accepting the new information, questioning it, or waiting for something more important.
