Why Gold and the Dollar Aren't Acting Like Havens

The AI-led sell-off is spreading, but the unusual part is outside equities — gold is falling, the dollar is rising and Treasury yields are retreating. That looks less like a flight to safety and more like a scramble for cash.

By Vincent · Singapore

22 Nov 2026 · 11 min read

The AI-led sell-off is spreading across markets. But the unusual part is what is happening outside equities: gold is falling, the dollar is rising and Treasury yields are retreating. This looks less like a straightforward flight to safety and more like a scramble for liquidity.

The Nasdaq Composite fell 10% last week, its worst weekly decline since March 2020. Friday alone brought another 5.3% drop. The S&P 500 also entered correction territory, while volatility surged.

The obvious explanation is concern about AI valuations and the enormous spending required to build the infrastructure behind the industry. But look beyond equities and the market becomes more interesting.

Gold fell approximately 9% for the week, reaching its lowest level in more than a year. Silver and other precious metals also sold off. The US dollar strengthened to a 19-month high, while the 10-year Treasury yield declined as investors moved into government bonds. That is not the usual picture of a clean flight to safety.

If investors are worried about growth and financial stability, why is gold falling? Why is the dollar rising while Treasury yields are declining? And what would tell us that the selling is finally running out of steam?

The answer may lie in the difference between seeking safety and simply raising cash.

The AI sell-off is becoming a broader de-risking event

The AI investment story is no longer just about whether Nvidia can sell more chips or whether Anthropic can attract customers. The bigger question is whether the enormous investment across the AI ecosystem can generate returns that justify its cost.

Hyperscalers are spending heavily on data centres, chips, networking and computing capacity. AI developers need access to that infrastructure, while investors and lenders provide the capital to fund the expansion. The European Central Bank's November Financial Stability Review highlighted growing financial connections across this ecosystem — off-balance-sheet commitments, debt financing and circular investment relationships. The ECB estimated that large US technology companies had more than US$4 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments as of the second quarter of 2026, and noted that free cash flow had turned negative for most hyperscalers as spending accelerated.

This does not mean AI demand is artificial or that the industry is destined to collapse. It does mean investors have to assess more than revenue growth: financing costs, future returns and the ability of companies to keep funding their expansion. Anthropic's IPO was another test of appetite — its shares initially traded well above the US$83 offer price, then fell back as the wider sell-off intensified. The listing raised substantial capital, yet it did not restore confidence across the AI trade.

The market is beginning to ask a harder question: how much capital can this industry absorb before investors demand clearer evidence of returns? When confidence in a major investment theme weakens, investors may reduce exposure across the entire chain rather than wait to identify which individual companies are most vulnerable. That is how a technology-sector correction can become a broader risk event.

Why gold is falling when investors are worried

Gold is often described as a safe-haven asset. But that does not mean it must rise whenever equities fall. During a disorderly sell-off, investors can sell gold for the same reason they sell stocks: they need cash, want to reduce leverage, or must meet losses elsewhere in their portfolios.

Gold also faces pressure from monetary policy. The Federal Reserve's October meeting minutes showed inflation remained a major concern, and markets were pricing a high probability of another rate increase in December. Higher expected interest rates raise the opportunity cost of holding gold, which pays no interest. A stronger dollar adds further pressure, because gold is priced internationally in USD. That creates a difficult combination for bullion:

  • Equities are falling, creating potential safe-haven demand.
  • Hawkish Fed expectations are increasing the opportunity cost of holding gold.
  • A stronger dollar is making gold more expensive for buyers using other currencies.
  • Investors facing losses elsewhere may be selling liquid assets to raise cash.

The result is that gold can fall alongside equities even when geopolitical and financial uncertainty remains elevated. Friday's price action suggests liquidity pressure and interest-rate expectations were dominating safe-haven demand. That distinction matters: if gold cannot attract buyers during a period of market stress, it may be telling us investors are still focused on raising cash rather than protecting purchasing power.

The dollar is rising — but it isn't a clean safe-haven signal

The dollar's behaviour is also more complicated than it first appears. The DXY reached a 19-month high during the sell-off, even as the US 10-year yield declined. The dollar was benefiting from a combination of risk reduction, demand for liquidity and expectations of further Fed tightening. But these forces do not necessarily point in the same direction over time.

A stronger dollar during a sell-off can reflect demand for USD liquidity. Investors, institutions and companies worldwide use dollars to settle transactions, fund positions and manage liabilities; when risk appetite deteriorates, demand for dollar liquidity can increase. At the same time, the Fed's hawkish outlook has supported the dollar relative to currencies where rate expectations are less aggressive.

Falling Treasury yields complicate the picture. If yields are declining because investors expect weaker growth and eventually a less restrictive Fed, the dollar's interest-rate advantage could narrow. If the dollar remains strong despite falling yields, liquidity demand and risk reduction may be doing more of the work. That is why I'd be careful about treating the dollar's current strength as a straightforward bullish signal.

There is also a Treasury detail worth keeping in perspective. On November 19, the US Treasury auctioned a US$15 billion, nine-day cash-management bill — a very short-term instrument used to manage temporary swings in the government's cash balance. Its existence alone is not evidence that investors are losing confidence in US debt; the auction attracted bids of nearly four times the amount offered. What matters more is the broader funding picture: demand at regular auctions, borrowing costs across maturities, and whether investors keep absorbing longer-dated issuance comfortably. The dollar may be benefiting from the search for liquidity, but that does not remove the longer-term questions around US borrowing and confidence in dollar assets.

Treasury bonds are rallying — what does that tell us?

The decline in the 10-year yield suggests investors are seeking exposure to government bonds as they reduce risk elsewhere. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when demand for existing Treasuries rises, prices rise and yields fall. But there are two different reasons yields can decline:

  • A safety bid — investors move into government bonds to protect capital during stress.
  • A growth and policy reassessment — investors expect weaker activity or a less aggressive future path for rates.

The two can happen at the same time. The important question is whether the bond rally is orderly and supported by healthy demand, or whether it is part of a wider scramble for liquidity. For now, falling yields alongside a stronger dollar and weaker gold suggests investors are not simply buying every traditional safe haven — they are choosing where to hold liquidity and reducing exposure to assets that may be vulnerable to further selling.

What this means for FX

The cross-asset picture matters because the same forces can produce different outcomes across currencies.

  • USD — supported, but not an uncomplicated buy. The dollar has benefited from liquidity demand and relatively hawkish US rate expectations. But if Treasury yields keep falling and the market begins pricing a weaker US growth outlook or a less aggressive Fed, that support could change. Watch whether the dollar stays strong once equity volatility starts to ease — if it does, the move may be more than a temporary liquidity bid.
  • JPY and CHF — watch for confirmation from risk sentiment. The yen and franc can attract safe-haven demand during stress, but their performance also depends on domestic rate expectations and the size of their yield disadvantages. A sustained recovery alongside falling equities would suggest investors are becoming more defensive; a reversal as volatility falls would suggest the initial safety demand is fading.
  • AUD and other risk-sensitive currencies — exposed to broader de-risking. If the Nasdaq sell-off spreads into a wider reduction in global risk appetite, currencies such as the Australian dollar may face pressure. But the FX response should not rest on equity performance alone — relative rate expectations, commodity prices and domestic conditions still matter.

The key is identifying whether the market is moving from an AI-specific repricing into a broader deterioration in risk appetite and growth expectations.

How do we know when the market is recovering?

A large sell-off does not end simply because prices bounce for a day. The first rebound can be short covering, bargain hunting or investors closing profitable defensive positions. A more durable recovery needs confirmation across several markets. The signals I'd watch:

  • The Nasdaq reclaims broken support. On the US Tech 100, the 26,200–26,700 area is the first zone to watch. The index has fallen below both levels and trades around 25,200. A recovery above 26,200 would be an initial improvement; reclaiming 26,700 and holding it on a pullback would be stronger. A rally that fails below former support is less convincing — old support can become resistance.
  • Market breadth improves. The index can rebound because a few large tech stocks recover, which doesn't mean the wider market is healthy. Watch advancers versus decliners, the equal-weighted S&P 500 relative to the cap-weighted index, and whether small and mid-caps participate.
  • Volatility begins to fall. The VIX surged during the sell-off. A sustained decline would suggest demand for downside protection is easing — but it should happen alongside improving breadth and steadier credit.
  • Credit markets stop deteriorating. This may be one of the most important signals. If investors are worried about the financing behind the AI buildout, watch corporate bond spreads, particularly for highly leveraged data-centre and infrastructure companies. A recovery in equities without stabilising credit spreads could be fragile.
  • Gold stops being sold during equity weakness. Gold doesn't need to rally immediately. But if equities stay volatile and gold starts holding its ground — and especially if it recovers while the dollar remains firm and real yields stay elevated — that would suggest buyers are returning for reasons beyond a weaker dollar or lower rates.
  • The dollar and yields become less one-sided. If yields fall and the dollar also weakens, the market may be moving away from the current liquidity and rate-differential trade. If yields fall but the dollar stays strong, defensive dollar demand is still dominant. A more stable relationship across bonds, the dollar and equities would signal the market is leaving its most disorderly phase.

The strongest recovery signal would be several of these conditions improving together — not just one green day on the Nasdaq.

The takeaway

This sell-off is not simply about Nvidia, Anthropic or one disappointing earnings report. It is about a market that has become heavily exposed to one investment theme, one group of large companies and an enormous wave of capital spending. The AI opportunity may be real — but the financing, valuations and expected returns still have to make sense.

At the same time, the Fed is keeping financial conditions tight, gold is being sold despite equity stress, and the dollar is benefiting from liquidity demand even as Treasury yields retreat. That combination tells us the market is still trying to decide what kind of risk event this is.

For FX traders, the recovery matters as much as the sell-off. If the Nasdaq stabilises, credit spreads narrow and volatility falls, risk-sensitive currencies may find support. If the dollar stays firm and defensive currencies keep attracting demand, the market may still be de-risking. Don't call the bottom because the Nasdaq bounces — look for confirmation across equities, bonds, gold, credit and currencies. That is how we distinguish a temporary relief rally from a genuine change in market behaviour.
