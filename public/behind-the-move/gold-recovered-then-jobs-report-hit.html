Gold Recovered. Then the US Jobs Report Hit. Why Didn't Buyers Walk Away?

Gold bounced ~4.5% off its October low, then a hot payroll print should have sent it lower — and buyers defended $4,040 instead. What that says about whether this is a bottom or just a breather.

9 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Gold ended October under heavy pressure. On October 30 it fell to around $3,876 an ounce, while silver dropped below $50. The sell-off looked like more than an ordinary correction: technology-sector concerns were triggering liquidations, and bullion was being sold to raise cash.

But the following week brought a sharp reversal. By Monday, November 9, gold was trading around $4,045–$4,055 — up roughly 4.5% from its October 30 low. Silver had recovered above $52, gaining even more from its sub-$50 trough.

The recovery was impressive. But the more revealing moment came on Friday.

The October US payroll report came in at 165,000 jobs, against expectations of just 65,000. Unemployment held at 4.1%, and previous months were revised higher. The dollar strengthened, Treasury yields moved back towards 5.15%, and expectations of a December Fed rate hike firmed. Gold initially fell towards $4,032 — then buyers stepped in, holding the $4,040 area and finishing the week strong.

That leaves one question: has the sell-off created a genuine buying opportunity, or are traders simply catching their breath before another decline?

The October sell-off was not just about gold

The October 30 decline happened alongside pressure across technology stocks and other financial markets. Investors were questioning the scale and financing of AI-related capital expenditure — concerns about spending, borrowing and future returns that contributed to selling in technology shares.

When portfolios come under pressure, investors sometimes sell assets that are relatively liquid, even if those assets were not the original source of the problem. Gold can become one of those assets. That helps explain why bullion fell so sharply: the selling was not necessarily a sudden rejection of gold's longer-term appeal — it may also have reflected investors raising cash and reducing exposure elsewhere.

Silver faced additional pressure because it has both precious-metal and industrial characteristics. When growth concerns intensify, its industrial exposure can make it more vulnerable than gold.

The important distinction is between why an asset is being sold and whether investors still want to own it. Those are not always the same thing.

Why gold and silver recovered

The first part of the recovery came as forced selling eased. Gold moved back above $4,000 while silver recovered more than 6% from its lows and reclaimed $52. Short-covering and dip-buying helped prices rebound after the sharp October liquidation.

Oil also pulled back — Brent crude moved from around $118 towards $112 — easing some immediate concerns about energy-driven inflation. And there was a political development: the US midterm elections pointed towards a divided Congress and the possibility of legislative gridlock. Markets appeared to take some comfort from the prospect of fewer large new fiscal spending initiatives, temporarily easing pressure on Treasury yields. That mattered for gold, because lower yields reduce the opportunity cost of holding an asset that pays no interest.

But this was only temporary relief. The US 10-year yield remained above 5%, and the market was still weighing the possibility that the Fed might raise rates again in December. The recovery did not happen because the macro environment suddenly became friendly to gold — it happened because some of the immediate selling pressure eased while buyers returned.

The jobs report should have been bad news for gold

Friday's payroll report complicated the recovery. The US added 165,000 jobs in October, well above the 65,000 expected, unemployment held at 4.1%, and earlier payroll figures were revised higher. That was evidence of a more resilient labour market than investors had anticipated.

A stronger labour market can give the Fed more room to keep interest rates elevated. If inflation also remains stubborn, the argument for another rate hike becomes stronger. The immediate market reaction reflected that:

  • Dollar recovered towards 102.16 on the DXY.
  • US 10-year yield moved back towards 5.12–5.15%.
  • December Fed rate-hike expectations firmed towards 75–80%.
  • Gold briefly dropped towards $4,032.

Yet gold did not collapse again. Buyers defended the $4,040 area, leaving it around $4,045–$4,055 on Monday.

That does not prove gold has regained its safe-haven momentum, nor that higher yields no longer matter. It tells us something more limited but still useful: the market was willing to buy gold even when the latest US data strengthened the case for higher interest rates. That could reflect dip-buying after the sharp sell-off, short-covering, continued precious-metals demand, or some combination — the price action alone cannot tell us which explanation will dominate next.

Silver's recovery is worth watching too

Silver has recovered from around $49.12 to approximately $51.90–$52.05. The gold/silver ratio has compressed to around 77.9, suggesting silver has outperformed gold during the rebound.

That matters because the two metals do not always respond to the same drivers. Gold is more directly influenced by real yields, monetary expectations, central-bank demand and defensive positioning. Silver carries all of that plus significant industrial exposure, which makes expectations for manufacturing and economic activity important.

If silver continues to outperform, it could suggest the recovery is broadening beyond defensive demand for gold. But one strong rebound is not enough to establish that industrial demand is improving — that would need further evidence from economic data and sustained relative strength.

The levels that matter now

The recovery has brought both metals back towards important resistance areas. The next move will help show whether buyers are rebuilding positions or simply covering shorts.

Gold (XAU/USD)

Immediate support $4,020–$4,035 · major structural support $3,875–$3,920 · immediate resistance $4,085–$4,126. Gold is holding above $4,040 after Friday's payroll reaction; a sustained move through $4,085–$4,126 would strengthen the recovery case, while a break below $4,020–$4,035 puts the structure under pressure.

Silver (XAG/USD)

Immediate support $51.20–$51.45 · major structural support $49.10–$50.00 · immediate resistance $52.40–$53.00. Silver has reclaimed $50 but now needs to hold its post-payroll base; a sustained move through $52.40–$53.00 would be further evidence buyers remain in control.

These are reference zones, not guaranteed turning points. The reaction around them matters more than the levels themselves.

This week's CPI could decide whether the recovery has legs

The next major test is the US October CPI report on Tuesday, November 10. Consensus is tracking headline inflation around 3.6% year-on-year. After the payroll surprise, the inflation report could have an outsized effect on expectations for the December Fed meeting.

  • If inflation runs hotter than expected — the market may price a greater chance of another Fed hike, supporting the dollar and yields and putting renewed pressure on gold and silver. The key question becomes whether gold can keep holding above $4,020 despite a more hawkish rate outlook.
  • If inflation comes in cooler — rate-hike expectations could ease, giving the metals more room to recover. A softer dollar and lower yields would make a sustained move through gold's $4,085–$4,126 resistance more plausible.

Either way, the reaction depends on the details and how markets read them, not just the headline number. US PPI and the second estimate of Eurozone Q3 GDP arrive on Friday, adding more data points before the week ends.

The lesson

A rebound is not the same as a bottom. A floor is only confirmed when selling pressure repeatedly fails to push prices lower.

Gold and silver have recovered sharply from their October lows, and that sell-off may have involved forced liquidation rather than a fundamental collapse in demand. But Friday's payroll report showed the macro headwinds haven't disappeared: the Fed may still have reason to keep rates high, yields remain elevated, and this week's CPI could reinforce or challenge current expectations.

For gold, the next test is whether buyers can keep defending $4,020–$4,035 and eventually push through $4,085–$4,126. For silver, it is whether the market holds above $51.20–$51.45 and challenges $52.40–$53.00. The October low may have marked capitulation — but you only know a floor held after it has been tested and held more than once.
