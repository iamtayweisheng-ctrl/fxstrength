Why Is the Fed Giving Markets Less Guidance? What Does That Mean for the Dollar?

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is questioning forward guidance — telling markets what the central bank might do next. Could less of it make the dollar harder to price?

1 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

The Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.75%–4.00% on October 28. The 10–2 vote included two officials who preferred an immediate 25-basis-point hike, and the committee upgraded its assessment of activity, describing growth as strong while acknowledging that inflation remained elevated.

But the press conference raised a broader question. Chair Kevin Warsh repeatedly avoided committing to a future rate path, emphasising that the Fed would respond to incoming information rather than market expectations. This wasn't simply a refusal to answer — it fits a communication philosophy he had already outlined in his August Jackson Hole speech. Warsh wants to change the role of forward guidance in monetary policy.

Why does Warsh want less forward guidance?

Forward guidance became a regular feature of Fed communication during the Global Financial Crisis. With rates near zero, telling markets that rates would stay low helped influence longer-term borrowing costs and financial conditions. But Warsh argues that this crisis-era practice has become too prominent in normal times.

His concern is that markets can begin treating the Fed's projections and comments as promises. If investors build positions around an expected rate path, the central bank may find it harder to change direction when the data shifts. He describes this as a "hall-of-mirrors" problem:

Markets rely on the Fed's guidance to form expectations. The Fed then watches market prices to assess conditions. If both become too dependent on each other's expectations, they risk missing what's actually happening in the economy.

His preferred approach is to give markets less of a promised roadmap and let them interpret economic information more independently.

Source: Federal Reserve — Warsh's August 28 Jackson Hole speech.

Is this about flexibility — or avoiding accountability?

There are two sides. The case for less guidance is straightforward: the economy is hard to forecast, and the Fed shouldn't make commitments that may become inappropriate when new information arrives. Warsh also argues that economic relationships change — a mechanical reaction function can't reliably tell policymakers exactly how to respond to every combination of inflation, growth and employment data.

But there's a counterargument. Forward guidance can help households, businesses and investors understand how policymakers are thinking. Removing too much of it may increase uncertainty, make market expectations more volatile, and complicate financial planning. There's also an accountability question: with fewer explanations of its future thinking, it may become harder for markets to distinguish a deliberate policy change from a reaction to the latest data.

Less guidance may give policymakers more room to manoeuvre. It does not automatically make monetary policy easier to understand.

The October meeting shows how this works in practice

Warsh's October press conference is a useful example. The Fed held rates, but two officials wanted an immediate hike. Warsh described activity as strong while warning that inflation remained frustratingly elevated, and highlighted higher energy prices and their relationship with market interest rates.

On AI investment, he rejected the idea that high interest rates no longer matter simply because technology companies are spending heavily on infrastructure — pointing to widening credit spreads in certain areas and arguing that financing costs still influence investment decisions.

Notice what he didn't do: he gave no specific promise about December. Instead, he explained the conditions the committee was watching and left the next decision open — consistent with the approach he outlined in August.

What does this mean for USD?

For FX traders, less forward guidance could make the relationship between economic data, Treasury yields and the dollar more important. Under a more explicit framework, investors may focus heavily on the Fed's projected rate path. Under Warsh's approach, they may need to pay closer attention to what the data implies about the next decision.

Consider three possible outcomes:

  • Strong growth and persistent inflation — markets may price a longer period of restrictive policy or further tightening, potentially supporting USD through higher expected yields.
  • Cooling inflation and weaker activity — rate expectations may move lower, pressuring the dollar.
  • Conflicting signals — the Fed may stay on hold while markets repeatedly adjust expectations, creating more volatility in Treasury yields and USD.

These are scenarios, not predictions. The important point: the absence of a promised rate path doesn't remove the Fed's influence — it changes how investors have to interpret it.

The lesson

Warsh isn't arguing that the Fed should stop communicating. He's arguing that communication should explain the policy framework without turning possible future decisions into quasi-commitments — preserving flexibility, reducing the market's dependence on Fed promises, and letting financial prices provide information about the economy. Whether this improves monetary policy remains an open question.

For FX traders, the practical issue is clear: if the Fed gives markets less of a roadmap, economic data and market pricing have to do more of the work. Watch how Treasury yields respond to inflation, employment and growth releases — not just what the Fed says at its next press conference.
