The Gulf Conflict Is Escalating. So Why Isn't Brent Rising?

The war risk is back, but oil isn't reacting the way it did during the initial shock. The reason lies in what has changed in the physical oil market — and it matters for the energy currencies.

20 Nov 2026 · 7 min read

When conflict escalates in the Gulf, the instinct is to expect oil prices to rise. The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical shipping route; tanker attacks and military exchanges raise the risk of further disruption. If a significant amount of oil can't reach the market, buyers may have to compete for fewer barrels.

But this time, Brent has not responded with another sustained rally. That isn't necessarily because the geopolitical risk has disappeared — the market is simply dealing with a different supply picture than it was during the initial disruption. Global production has recovered sharply, Gulf exports have adapted, demand has weakened, and the International Energy Agency now expects the global oil market to return to surplus in the fourth quarter of 2026.

The conflict is escalating, but the immediate shortage of crude is easing. That changes how the market weighs each new headline.

The market has more oil available than during the initial shock

The latest IEA figures show a substantial recovery in supply. Global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels per day in October to 105.4 mb/d, following a 3 mb/d increase in September. Gulf exports have also recovered faster than expected: in October, Gulf oil exports excluding Oman reached 18.4 mb/d — still 26% below pre-war levels, but crude flows had recovered to around 82% of their pre-war level.

This matters because oil prices respond to the amount of supply the market believes is available, not simply to whether a conflict is taking place. During the initial disruption, a large volume of production and exports was unavailable, so buyers competed for fewer barrels and prices reflected that scarcity. Now, more supply is reaching the market through recovering exports and alternative shipping arrangements. The conflict remains a threat — but the market is no longer dealing with the same degree of immediate supply loss.

Demand is weakening at the same time

Supply recovery is only half the explanation. Demand has also become less supportive of higher prices. The IEA forecasts global oil demand will fall by 2.7 mb/d in 2026, to 102.3 mb/d, with high fuel prices, weaker industrial activity, lower refinery runs and efficiency improvements all contributing.

This creates an important change in the balance: if supply recovers while demand weakens, the market can move from scarcity towards surplus even while geopolitical risks remain elevated. The IEA expects supply to exceed demand in Q4. That doesn't mean every region has enough oil, or that every fuel is readily available — it means the overall crude picture has improved enough for the market to become less sensitive to each new disruption headline.

The crude market is recovering, but the energy system isn't back to normal

There's an important detail that gets lost when we focus only on Brent: the recovery in crude exports has been faster than the recovery in refined products and LPG. The IEA says refined-product and LPG shipments from the Middle East remain far below pre-war levels, with diesel markets particularly tight as lost exports from the Middle East and Russia continue to affect availability.

Inventories tell a more complicated story too. Observed global oil inventories rose by 52 million barrels in October — but much of that came from oil held at sea as exports recovered. On-land stocks continued to fall and remain at their lowest since at least 2016. More crude is moving through the system, but the buffer available on land is still thin.

This distinction matters because crude and refined products aren't interchangeable at the point of consumption. A refinery needs the right crude grade, the capacity to process it, and the ability to deliver the products to the right market. A recovery in crude supply can therefore ease Brent before diesel, transport fuel and other energy markets return to normal.

Brent can look more comfortable while parts of the physical energy system remain under pressure.

The market is pricing a surplus, not declaring the Gulf safe

The IEA's Q4 surplus outlook helps explain why Brent hasn't pushed higher with every new military development. The market is looking at the expected balance between supply and demand over the coming months. If traders believe Gulf exports can keep recovering, alternative supply can compensate for disruptions, and demand will stay weak, then the immediate need to pay a large scarcity premium declines.

That doesn't remove the geopolitical risk premium — it means the premium is being weighed against a more comfortable supply outlook. There's also a difference between a headline risk and a confirmed loss of supply: a tanker attack increases uncertainty, but a sustained reduction in exports would have a more direct effect on the physical balance. For a lasting Brent rally, the market may need evidence that the recovery in Gulf exports is reversing — not simply another escalation. That's why the next export and inventory figures may matter more than the daily sequence of military headlines.

What this means for currencies

The change in the oil market creates different effects across currencies — and it interacts with which central banks can actually afford to keep fighting inflation.

USD — rate expectations still matter more than oil

The dollar has support from firm US rate expectations and Treasury yields. Lower oil may eventually reduce some headline inflation pressure, but the pass-through to consumer prices takes time. If the market keeps expecting further Fed tightening, that can remain a more immediate driver than a modest decline in crude. The dollar also has a separate source of support during geopolitical uncertainty: demand for liquidity. So a softer Brent does not automatically mean a weaker USD.

EUR and JPY — some relief for energy importers

The Eurozone and Japan are major energy importers, so lower crude can reduce their import bills and ease some headline energy inflation — a helpful shift in their terms of trade. But it's only one part of the picture. The euro remains sensitive to growth expectations and rate differentials; the yen remains driven by the US–Japan yield gap and BOJ policy expectations. Cheaper oil removes one headwind; it doesn't automatically reverse the others.

CAD and NOK — less support from crude

The Canadian dollar and Norwegian krone can draw support from stronger energy prices, given their energy exposure. When crude falls on surplus expectations, that support weakens. But the relationship isn't mechanical — domestic data, central-bank expectations and broad USD moves can still dominate. The key is distinguishing a currency losing an oil-price tailwind from a currency entering a broader downtrend.

What would make Brent rise again?

The current balance is more comfortable, but not immune to another supply shock. Several developments are worth watching:

  • Gulf export flows. Do crude exports keep recovering, or do tanker attacks and shipping restrictions begin to reverse the improvement?
  • Inventories. Are global stocks still building — and crucially, are on-land inventories recovering, or is the apparent improvement mostly oil held at sea?
  • The physical product market. Diesel and other refined products remain tight; if product shortages worsen, they could create broader energy-market stress even with adequate crude.
  • Demand. Does weaker demand keep offsetting recovering supply? A stronger-than-expected consumption recovery could shrink the projected surplus.
  • The scale of disruption. A localised incident creates volatility; a sustained loss of meaningful export volume would challenge the surplus outlook and could force a rapid repricing.

The takeaway

The Gulf conflict hasn't become irrelevant to oil prices — the market has simply changed what it's focusing on. During the initial shock, the dominant concern was how much supply could disappear. Now, the question is whether recovering exports, alternative supply and weaker demand can keep the global balance comfortable despite continued disruption. That's why Brent can stay subdued even as the conflict escalates.

But there's a risk in reading too much into the headline crude price. Refined-product shortages, high freight costs and depleted on-land inventories show the energy system is still fragile. For FX traders, the same distinction matters: lower oil can ease the terms-of-trade and inflation pressure on importers while reducing support for exporters — but currencies will still respond to relative rate expectations, growth, and the dollar's own demand drivers.

Geopolitical risk doesn't automatically translate into higher oil prices. The market needs to see a credible threat to actual supply — and right now, recovering flows and weaker demand are pushing in the opposite direction.
