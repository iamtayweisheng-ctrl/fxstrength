What is a gold strength meter?

XAU/USD only tells you gold's story against the dollar. If the dollar is weak everywhere, gold can look like it's rallying when really the dollar is just falling. A gold strength meter fixes that by scoring gold against the whole basket of majors — USD, EUR, JPY, GBP and the rest — and placing it on the same scale as the currencies. That separates gold strength from dollar weakness, which a single XAU/USD chart can't do.

How is gold strength calculated?

The method mirrors currency strength: take gold's percentage move against each major currency over a chosen window and average them into one score. The twist is scale. Gold routinely moves more than the fiats in a day, so putting it on the raw currency scale would peg it at the extreme almost every session. Instead, the meter normalises gold to its own typical volatility, so its 0–10 score reads "how strong is gold versus its own normal," not against the currency yardstick. A 9/10 for gold means a strong day for gold — it is not the same as a 9/10 for the dollar.

What moves gold?

Gold doesn't pay interest, so its biggest swing factor is the real yield — the return on cash and bonds after inflation. When real yields rise, holding gold costs more in forgone interest and gold tends to soften; when they fall, gold's appeal rises. Around that sit three forces worth watching on the meter:

  • The US dollar. Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar is often a headwind — but the meter shows whether gold is weak outright or merely weak against a strong dollar.
  • Risk sentiment and havens. In stress, gold can attract defensive demand alongside the yen and franc — or not, depending on whether the move is a liquidity scramble (which can favour the dollar instead).
  • Central banks and inflation. Policy expectations and official buying shift the longer-run backdrop.

These forces often pull in different directions at once, which is why gold's price alone can mislead. A few worked examples:

  • The hot CPI paradox: why yields fell and gold rose — the real-yield link in action.
  • Gold recovered, then the US jobs report hit — why buyers didn't walk away.
  • Gold has broken down — where will buyers finally step in?
  • Oil is surging — why don't oil and gold move together?

How do I read the gold score?

A high score means gold has been strong relative to its own recent range; a low score, weak. Check the direction arrow for whether that strength is building or fading, and the timeframe for whether it's an intraday blip or a weekly trend. When gold is strong on all three timeframes at once, that's the highest-conviction read. And because the score is normalised to gold's volatility, compare it to gold's own history — not directly to a currency's number.
